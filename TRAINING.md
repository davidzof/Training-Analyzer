# Introduction

This article reconciles the various ways endurance training intensity
is described: physiological domains, lactate and ventilatory thresholds,
power zones, heart-rate zones, RPE and the familiar
3-, 5- and 7-zone systems. 

The underlying physiology is continuous and individual,
while the zone systems are mostly practical communication tools
layered on top of it.

## Training zones are more complicated than they appear

Endurance training is simultaneously quite simple and surprisingly
imprecise. At a practical level, the recipe is familiar:

* train consistently
* do a lot of relatively easy work
* add some intensity
* recover properly

But sport science is much less sure about exactly which
intensity produces which adaptation in a particular individual.

## Durability

The classic Joyner model describes endurance performance through
three main variables:

* VO₂max
* exercise economy - the metabilic cost of a given workload
* the fraction of VO₂max that can be sustained around threshold

Durability adds another dimension: **how well those characteristics are preserved after several hours of exercise**.
The last 10K of a marathon, the final climb of a bike ride, the summit
push at the end of a long day back country skiing. This is the difference
between success and failure.

Two riders can have identical VO₂max, economy and threshold when fresh,
but after three or five hours one may retain them much better
than the other. That degradation is durability.

Athletes and coaches have always understood this in practice:
the relevant question is not who can produce the best power when fresh,
but who can still produce it late in a race.

There is no agreed way to measure durability and little evidence
about the optimal way to train it, although high lifetime training
volume and putting harder efforts late in long rides are
strategies.

This also introduces an important idea that returns later:
**your zones themselves are not completely static**. After many hours
or several days of riding, the power corresponding to a physiological
domain may fall.

## What do we mean by a training zone?

There is a difference between **external workloads** and the
**internal physiological response**.

For example:

* Power tells you what mechanical work you are producing.
* Heart rate tells you how the cardiovascular system is responding.
* Lactate tells you something about metabolic state.
* Ventilation and gas exchange tell you about whole-body respiratory/metabolic response.
* RPE tells you how hard the effort feels.

Training zones are attempts to organize those measurements into useful intensity categories. They have two purposes:

1. to prescribe training;
2. to quantify how the body responded to that training.

Different metrics describe different aspects
of the same effort.

## Heart rate based training

Heart rate is attractive because it is cheap and easy to measure.

Its advantage and disadvantage are two sides of the same coin:
**heart rate measures the integrated response of the body**.

It is influenced not only by exercise intensity but also by:

* heat and humidity
* dehydration
* fatigue
* stress
* caffeine
* altitude
* illness
* glycogen status

Consequently, a lower HR at the same power could mean improved fitness,
or it could mean suppressed HR caused by excessive fatigue.

A second issue is that HR is a lagging indicator. It may take a number of
seconds to react to metabolic changes.

## Power

Cyclists are unusually fortunate because a power meter directly measures
mechanical work.

Its big advantage is that **200 W is 200 W**, irrespective of gradient, wind or speed.

But this is also its limitation. The physiological cost of producing 200 W
can differ dramatically depending on whether it is minute 10 or hour 5,
whether the rider is fresh or fatigued, and whether the environmental
conditions are benign or stressful.

So power is an excellent measure of external load, but it does not directly tell you the internal metabolic state.
It does have the advantage in reacting instantly to changes.

## RPE

**RPE, rating of perceived exertion**, is extremely useful
but difficult to calibrate.

The advantage is obvious: it requires no equipment. The difficulty is
distinguishing reliably between, say, RPE 5 and RPE 6.

## Lactate testing and VO₂

Lactate testing is much more practical than full metabolic testing but
still requires blood samples and is vulnerable to both measurement
and interpretation errors.

**Spiroergometry**, measures oxygen uptake, O₂ consumption, CO₂ production and
ventilation. This provides much richer information because it allows
the examination of:

* VO₂
* CO₂ production
* ventilation
* respiratory exchange ratio
* fat/carbohydrate substrate use
* ventilatory thresholds

Gas exchange is seen as the gold standard for measuring physiological
processes occurring during exercise, but traditional laboratory equipment makes it impractical for everyday training. 
Devices such as Pairfs and Tymewear are attempts to take this kind of lab
analysis into the field.

## LT1, VT1, LT2 and VT2

* **LT1** = first lactate threshold
* **VT1** = first ventilatory threshold

are closely related manifestations of the transition from the
**moderate** to the **heavy** exercise domain.

Likewise:

* **LT2**
* **VT2**
* MLSS
* FTP
* Critical Power

are all, in practical terms, attempts to identify the transition from
the **heavy** to the **severe** domain.

These are not  identical physiological quantities, but from the athlete's
point of view they are all trying
to describe roughly the same important upper transition.

That gives the classic three physiological domains:

**Moderate → Heavy → Severe**

with LT1/VT1 separating moderate from heavy and LT2/VT2 separating heavy from severe.

## Why the 3-zone model is physiologically attractive

The **3-zone model** is elegant because its boundaries correspond
reasonably closely to genuine physiological transitions.

In simple terms:

| 3-zone model | Physiology                         |
| ------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| Zone 1       | Below LT1/VT1 — moderate domain    |
| Zone 2       | Between LT1 and LT2 — heavy domain |
| Zone 3       | Above LT2/VT2 — severe domain      |

That makes it conceptually cleaner than the 5- or 7-zone systems.

Its disadvantage is that it can be too coarse for detailed training prescription.

For example, recovery riding and fairly strong endurance
riding may both fall into Zone 1 of the three-zone model
despite being obviously different training intensities.

## Why “Zone 2” can be confusing

**You cannot talk about “Zone 2” without saying which zone
system you using.**

Zone 2 in the three-zone model is the **heavy domain between LT1 and LT2**.

Zone 2 in the Coggan-style five-zone model is generally **below LT1** and corresponds to easy endurance training.

They are therefore completely different physiological intensities.

This is a major source of confusion in discussions about “Zone 2 training”.

## The 5- and 7-zone models

The Coggan/Allen-type systems as primarily organised around the
second threshold.

Typically the lower zones are something like:

* Zone 1: recovery
* Zone 2: endurance
* Zone 3: tempo
* Zone 4: threshold

with higher zones divided into VO₂max, anaerobic and sprint work.

These systems are useful because they give coaches more descriptive resolution than the 3-zone system.

But the borders are partly arbitrary population averages rather than physiological discontinuities.

For example, saying that Zone 2 ends at 70% or 75% of FTP does **not** imply that every athlete reaches LT1 at that percentage. 

## The heart-rate zone problem

Zones calculated simply from percentages of maximum HR can be problematic

The worst approach is estimating HRmax using any of the myriad of
formulas such as **220 − age**.

Using an actually measured sport-specific HRmax is better. HR reserve improves things further.

But neither tells you where LT1 or LT2 actually occurs.
Ideally HR zones should be anchored to individually measured HR
at LT1/VT1 and LT2/VT2.


| 5-zone model | Physiology                              |
|--------------|-----------------------------------------|
| Zone 1       |                                         |
| Zone 2       | Below LT1/VT1                           |
| Zone 3       | Lower half of >LT1/VT1                  |
| Zone 4       | Upper half of > LT1/VT1 |
| Zone 5       | Above LT2/VT2           |

## Fixed percentages don't work

Population wide percentages or value used to calculate thresholds are
frequently inaccurate for individuals:

* lactate concentrations e.g. 2mmol/l
* FatMax
* percentage of VO₂peak
* percentage of HRmax

Rule such as:

> LT1 = 70% FTP

or

> Zone 2 = 60–70% HRmax

may work reasonably well as a population-level approximation, but can be badly wrong for an individual athlete.

Similarly, fixed lactate concentrations such as 2 mmol/L or 4 mmol/L should
not be assumed to represent the same physiological threshold in everybody.

## Lactate testing itself is surprisingly uncertain

With lactate testing **the method used to identify the threshold
matters enormously**.

For a start the tests result in a number of discrete points to which a
curve is fitted. There are several methods to calculate LT1 and LT2:

* 2 mmol/l and 4 mmol/l
* 0.3 mmol/l above baseline
* 1 mmol/l above baseline
* Least-squares polynomial regression

Each of these produces dramatically different
threshold values and that is if you end up with a textbook
graph of blood lactate.

A laboratory test is not automatically a gold standard simply because
it involves expensive equipment.

Protocol, stage duration, sampling, mathematical interpretation and
operator all matter. 

## Metcart CPET  (Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing) Testing

CPET is a good example of the difference between well understood
physiology and imperfect measurement. The underlying physiological
response to increasing exercise intensity is relatively clear:
below the first threshold, ventilation and lactate remain fairly stable;
around VT1, ventilation begins to rise disproportionately to
oxygen uptake as buffering generates additional CO₂;
around VT2 or the respiratory compensation point, ventilation increases
more sharply as the body tries to maintain a neutral blood pH.
The difficulty is that these transitions rarely appear as perfectly
clean breakpoints on a real test. VE/VO₂, VE/VCO₂, PETO₂, PETCO₂, RER
and raw ventilation may all change at slightly different points,
while breath-by-breath data are inherently noisy.

For that reason, identifying VT1 and VT2 is partly an exercise in
pattern recognition and interpretation rather than exact detection.

Two experienced observers may therefore place a threshold a few beats
per minute or a few watts apart without either necessarily being wrong.
The physiological transition is real, but the precise number assigned to
it is an estimate of a transition region.

## Finding the real upper boundary of Zone 2

How do we find the **upper limit of endurance
Zone 2 in the 5-zone system**, which corresponds roughly
to **LT1/VT1**.

Directly identifying this boundary is much better than
deriving it as a percentage of FTP.

Why?

Because you have two uncertainties otherwise:

1. FTP/LT2 itself may be incorrectly estimated.
2. The relationship between LT1 and LT2 differs substantially between
people.

So even a perfectly measured FTP does not automatically give you a
reliable LT1.

###  Exact precision is not essential

If LT1 is around 200 W or 140 bpm the training adaptation is
unlikely to disappear at 201 W or 141 bpm.

Likewise, riding slightly below the upper boundary will still produce
a useful endurance stimulus.

Thresholds are transitions, not magical switches.

Identifying LT1 accurately is therefore less about
riding exactly one watt below it and more about knowing roughly where
the **moderate → heavy transition** sits.

## What experts mean by Zone 2

Experts broadly preferred an intensity **just below LT1/VT1**, characterised by:

* physiological steady state
* little or only transient rise in lactate
* stable heart rate
* low RPE
* sustainable breathing

Crucially, the experts also acknowledge that the adaptations expected from Zone 2 are **probably not unique to that narrow intensity**. Slightly easier or slightly harder exercise may produce many of the same adaptations.


## Why LT1 matters particularly for ultra-endurance athletes

LT1/VT1 is arguably more useful than LT2 for bikepacking and ultra-endurance.

The reason is simple: the overwhelming majority of training and racing time occurs around or below that first threshold.

It is effectively the **“all-day” intensity domain**.

Consequently, he sees two useful objectives:

1. know where LT1/VT1 currently lies;
2. over time, try to push the sustainable power at LT1/VT1 upward.

That is quite different from the cycling culture that tends to treat FTP as the central measure of fitness. 

## 20. Practical ways of estimating LT1/VT1

**Nasal breathing** is sometimes suggested but that may be too individually
variable to be particularly reliable.

The **talk test** is better. Below VT1, speaking complete sentences should remain easy. Around VT1, conversation becomes noticeably less comfortable.
Above it, speech becomes broken.

**HR drift test**. Ride at a constant power for roughly 40–60 minutes
after warming up and compare HR in the first and second halves.

If HR rises by less than about **5%**, the workload is probably at or below
the aerobic threshold. Greater drift suggests you may be above it, assuming
confounding factors such as heat and dehydration are controlled.

Personally I favour a breathing test. LT1 is where breathing starts to increase
faster than effort due to excess CO2 but you can still continue for a long period.
LT2 is where breathing becomes very rapid and the effort can only continue from
seconds to under an hour (at LT2).

---

### Summary

**Physiology**

Moderate
↓ **LT1 / VT1**
Heavy
↓ **LT2 / VT2**
Severe

**Training-zone systems are then descriptive subdivisions layered over
those domains.**

So in a typical cycling 5-zone system:

**Z1 + Z2** ≈ moderate domain
**Z3 + much of Z4** ≈ heavy domain
**high Z4 / Z5+** ≈ severe domain

but the exact correspondence varies between individuals.


> **Measure LT1 and LT2 directly if you can, rather than assuming they occur at fixed percentages of FTP or HRmax. Then use power, HR and RPE together rather than treating any one metric as absolute truth.**

A  physiological 5-zone system should be built **around the measured
thresholds**, rather than allowing an arbitrary percentage-of-HRmax
system to put VT2 somewhere in the middle of Zone 4.
